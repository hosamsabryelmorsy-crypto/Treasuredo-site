Aviator Sunglasses History: Where the Shape Came From and How to Spot a Vintage Pair

Aviator sunglasses were developed in the 1930s for pilots: the large teardrop lens covers the eye's full field of view, including when looking down at instruments. Bausch & Lomb developed them for US military pilots and sold them to the public as Ray-Ban from 1937. Since then nearly every eyewear maker has produced aviators, and vintage pairs are identified by their maker's markings, lens material and construction.

Parts of an aviator frame

  • Teardrop lens: deeper at the outer bottom corner to cover downward vision.
  • Double bridge: a top bar ("sweat bar" or "brow bar") above the main bridge, adding strength.
  • Thin metal rims: originally gold-tone or silver-tone.
  • Adjustable nose pads on metal pad arms.
  • Temples: straight "bayonet" temples, or curved "cable" temples that wrap behind the ear for a secure fit.

Variations you will see

  • Navigator: a squarer lens with a flatter top.
  • Oversized 1970s aviators: lenses of 60 mm and more, often gradient.
  • Plastic aviators: acetate fronts in the aviator shape, popular in the 1980s.
  • Technical aviators: folding frames and interchangeable lenses, as on vintage Porsche Design and Carrera models.
  • European aviators: German makers such as Metzler and Rodenstock made many metal aviators in the 1970s-80s.

How to tell a vintage aviator from a modern one

  1. Lens material: many older aviators have glass lenses, which feel cool and heavier than plastic.
  2. Markings: maker's name, model number, size and country printed or engraved on the frame, often worn after decades.
  3. Logo on the lens: some brands etched a small mark near the edge; its style changed over the years.
  4. Construction: solder joints at the bridge and rims, screws in the hinges and rim locks.
  5. Wear: plating wear at the bridge and temple tips is consistent with honest use.

Sizing an aviator

Aviator sizes are given as lens width in millimetres. Common sizes are around 52, 55, 58 and 62 mm. As a rough guide, 52-55 mm suits narrow and medium faces and 58 mm and up suits wider faces or a deliberately oversized look. The bridge width (the second number) matters too: aviators with adjustable pads are more forgiving than fixed-bridge plastic frames.

What to check before buying

On metal aviators, look at the solder points of the double bridge and the rim locks next to the hinges, the two places most likely to crack. Check that the lenses match in tint, especially on gradient lenses. Cable temples should hold their curve; if they are stretched straight, an optician can usually reshape them.

General checks before you buy

  1. Hinges: open and close each temple; it should move smoothly with no wobble. Loose screws can be tightened, cracked hinge areas cannot.
  2. Frame material: look for hairline cracks, white haze or shrinkage, especially at the bridge and near the hinges.
  3. Lenses: check for scratches, peeling coatings or delamination at the edges. Sun lenses can be replaced, but original lenses add value.
  4. Size: compare lens width, bridge and temple length with a pair you already wear; a difference of 2-3 mm in lens width is noticeable.
  5. Straightness: place the frame upside down on a table; both temples should touch the surface evenly.

Frequently asked questions

Why were aviator sunglasses invented?

For pilots in the 1930s; the teardrop lens covers the eye’s full field of view.

What is the bar on top of aviator sunglasses called?

The brow bar, top bar or sweat bar; it forms the double bridge.

What size aviator sunglasses should I get?

Around 52-55 mm lenses suit narrow to medium faces; 58 mm and up suit wider faces.

What are cable temples?

Curved temples that wrap behind the ear, used on aviators for a secure fit.
