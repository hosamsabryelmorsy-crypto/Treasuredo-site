Browline Sunglasses and Glasses: History, Construction and How to Spot a Vintage Pair

Browline frames have a heavy upper rim, the "brow", usually in acetate, over a thin metal lower rim that holds the lens, so the frame follows the line of the eyebrows. The style was introduced in the late 1940s, with Shuron's Ronsir of 1947 the best-known early model, and was one of the most common frame styles in America in the 1950s and early 1960s. It came back in the 1980s and has stayed in production since.

How a browline frame is built

  • Brow pieces: two acetate or metal tops, one over each lens, screwed or riveted to the metal chassis.
  • Metal chassis: a thin metal frame that runs under each lens and across the bridge, often with adjustable nose pads on pad arms.
  • Rim screw: the lens is held by tightening the metal rim, usually with a small screw at the outer edge hidden under the brow.
  • Rivets: decorative or structural rivets on the brow are a classic detail.
  • Temples: usually acetate to match the brow, attached with hinges on the brow piece or chassis.

Vintage browline markings

American browlines of the 1950s and 1960s are often stamped on the bridge or chassis with the maker's name and a metal marking, for example "1/10 12K GF", meaning a layer of 12-carat gold, one-tenth of the metal's weight, bonded to a base metal. European browlines from the 1970s and 1980s, for example from Metzler in Germany, usually show the brand, model number and size inside the temple.

What to check before buying

  1. Brow to chassis joint: the screws or rivets that join the acetate brow to the metal frame should be tight, with no cracks in the acetate around them.
  2. Lens fit: lenses should not rotate or move; a loose rim screw is easy to fix, a cracked rim is not.
  3. Bridge solder: look for dark lines at the joints of the metal bridge.
  4. Gold-filled wear: on gold-filled chassis, wear shows as a darker base metal at the nose pads and rim edges.
  5. Nose pads: often yellowed or missing on old frames; easy and cheap to replace.

Sunglasses or optical frame?

Many vintage browlines were made as prescription frames and are sold with clear lenses. Because the lens is held in a full metal rim, any optician can fit tinted sun lenses or prescription lenses. Check the size inside a temple or on the bridge (lens width and bridge, for example 48□22) before having lenses made.

Who browlines suit

The strong brow adds definition to round and oval faces and gives a classic look on most faces. Common vintage sizes are smaller than modern frames, often 46-50 mm lens width, so compare with glasses you already wear. For a more modern size, 1980s and later browlines are usually larger.

General checks before you buy

  1. Hinges: open and close each temple; it should move smoothly with no wobble. Loose screws can be tightened, cracked hinge areas cannot.
  2. Frame material: look for hairline cracks, white haze or shrinkage, especially at the bridge and near the hinges.
  3. Lenses: check for scratches, peeling coatings or delamination at the edges. Sun lenses can be replaced, but original lenses add value.
  4. Size: compare lens width, bridge and temple length with a pair you already wear; a difference of 2-3 mm in lens width is noticeable.
  5. Straightness: place the frame upside down on a table; both temples should touch the surface evenly.

Frequently asked questions

What is a browline frame?

A frame with a heavy upper rim, usually acetate, over a thin metal lower rim, following the line of the eyebrows.

When were browline glasses popular?

They were introduced in the late 1940s and were very popular in the 1950s and early 1960s, returning in the 1980s.

What does 1/10 12K GF mean on vintage frames?

It means a layer of 12-carat gold making up one-tenth of the metal’s weight is bonded to a base metal.

Can I put sun lenses in a vintage browline frame?

Yes, an optician can fit sun or prescription lenses in the metal rim.
