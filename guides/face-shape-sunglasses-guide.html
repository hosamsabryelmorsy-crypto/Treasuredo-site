Which Sunglasses Suit My Face Shape? A Guide Using Vintage Frames

The simplest rule is contrast: round faces suit angular frames, square faces suit round or curved frames, and oval faces suit almost anything. Size matters as much as shape: the frame should be about as wide as your face at the temples, with your eyes near the centre of each lens. Vintage frames come in every shape, so face shape is a good way to narrow your choice.

How to find your face shape

Stand in front of a mirror with your hair pulled back. Compare the width of your forehead, cheekbones and jaw, and the length of your face.

  • Round: width and length similar, soft jaw, full cheeks.
  • Square: strong, angular jaw, forehead and jaw about the same width.
  • Oval: longer than wide, cheekbones widest, balanced features.
  • Heart: wider forehead, narrower chin.
  • Long: clearly longer than wide, often with a high forehead.

What suits each face shape

  • Round: square, rectangular and geometric frames, such as octagons and navigators, add structure. Browlines work well. Avoid very small round frames.
  • Square: round, panto and oval frames, aviators and cat-eyes soften angles. Avoid heavy square frames.
  • Oval: nearly all shapes; keep the frame in proportion to your face width.
  • Heart: frames wider at the bottom, light rims, aviators, round frames. Avoid very heavy tops.
  • Long: deeper lenses and oversized frames shorten the face; decorative temples add width.

Checking size on a vintage frame

  1. Read the numbers inside the temple: lens width, bridge width and sometimes temple length, in millimetres. See our guide to frame size numbers.
  2. Compare with a pair you own that fits well; this is more reliable than any face-shape rule.
  3. Total front width: measure your pair hinge to hinge, and compare with the listing if given.
  4. Bridge: a narrow bridge sits higher on the nose; a wide one lower. Frames with adjustable nose pads are more forgiving.
  5. Temple length: 135-145 mm is typical; acetate temples can be adjusted by an optician.

Fit checks once they are on

  • Eyes near the centre of each lens, not at the edge.
  • Eyebrows roughly following the top of the frame.
  • No pressure at the temples, no gap of more than a finger width.
  • Frame does not touch cheeks when you smile.
  • Frame does not slide down when you look down.

Vintage frames are often smaller

Frames from the 1950s and 1960s are often narrower than modern ones, while 1970s and 1980s frames are often larger. If you have a wide face, look at 1970s-80s oversized and aviator styles; for narrow faces, 1950s-60s frames and small metal frames from makers such as Neostyle often fit well.

Colour and material also change the look

  • Dark, thick acetate makes a frame look bigger and stronger on the face.
  • Thin metal or translucent acetate looks lighter and suits smaller faces and delicate features.
  • Tortoise and honey tones suit most skin tones and are a safe first vintage pair.
  • Gold-tone metal flatters warm skin tones; silver and gunmetal suit cool tones.
  • Gradient lenses soften a large frame, because the lighter lower half shows more of the face.

Frequently asked questions

What sunglasses suit a round face?

Angular frames such as square, rectangular, octagonal and browline frames.

What sunglasses suit a square face?

Round, panto, oval, aviator and cat-eye frames soften angular features.

How do I know if sunglasses are too big?

Your eyes should be near the centre of each lens and the frame about as wide as your face at the temples.

Are vintage sunglasses smaller than modern ones?

1950s-60s frames are often smaller; 1970s-80s frames are often larger.
