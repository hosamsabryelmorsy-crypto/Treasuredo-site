Can You Put New Lenses in Vintage Sunglasses? What an Optician Can and Cannot Do

Yes, most vintage sunglasses can take new lenses. An optician traces the shape of the frame, or of the old lens, and cuts new sun or prescription lenses to fit. Full-rim metal and acetate frames are the easiest; rimless and semi-rimless frames, shields, and very old celluloid frames need more care. Keep the original lenses if they have a maker's logo, since they add value.

How easy is your frame to re-lens?

  • Full-rim metal: easiest. The rim opens with a small screw at the outer edge, the new lens goes in, the screw is tightened.
  • Full-rim acetate: easy. The frame is warmed so it stretches slightly and the lens is pushed in; good acetate handles this well.
  • Semi-rimless: the lens is grooved around the edge and held by a nylon thread; new lenses need the same groove.
  • Rimless: the lens is drilled and screwed to the frame; hole positions must match exactly.
  • Shield and wrap: difficult; the lens must match a strong curve and a unique shape.
  • Old celluloid: risky. Pre-1960s celluloid can be brittle or shrink, and heating can damage it.

What to ask the optician

  1. Can the new lens match the original base curve, so it sits correctly in the rim?
  2. Will the lens have full UV protection?
  3. Which tint and darkness do you want: grey, brown, green, gradient?
  4. Do you want glass or plastic? Glass matches the original feel of many vintage pairs; plastic is lighter.
  5. Can they keep the original lenses for you?

Prescription lenses in vintage frames

Most full-rim vintage frames can take prescription lenses. Check the lens size first: vintage frames are often small, which is fine for most prescriptions, but very large oversized frames with strong prescriptions can make lenses thick at the edges. Make sure the frame sits straight and the temples are adjusted before the optician measures your pupil distance, so the lenses are centred correctly.

When to keep the original lenses

  • Branded glass lenses with an etched logo.
  • Rare tints or gradients matching the frame colour.
  • Lenses in good condition with no scratches in the centre.

If you replace them, store the originals in a soft pouch; a future buyer may value the pair more with them.

Signs your lenses need replacing

  • Scratches or haze in the centre of vision.
  • Peeling mirror or anti-reflective coatings.
  • Uneven tint between left and right lenses.
  • Chips at the edges of glass lenses near the rim screw.
  • Unknown UV protection, which an optician can check with a UV meter.

Before you take a frame to the optician

  1. Check the frame first: cracks at the bridge or hinges get worse when a frame is warmed or stressed for new lenses; fix or reconsider before paying for lenses.
  2. Tighten loose hinge screws so the frame can be measured straight.
  3. Bring the original lenses even if scratched; they give the exact shape and curve.
  4. Ask about the lens edge: for thin metal rims, the lens edge must be bevelled to sit in the groove.
  5. Ask for the frame to be adjusted after fitting, so it sits level on your face.

Frequently asked questions

Can you put new lenses in old sunglasses?

Yes, an optician can cut new sun or prescription lenses to fit most vintage frames.

Which vintage frames are hardest to re-lens?

Rimless, shield and wrap frames, and old celluloid frames that may be brittle.

Can I put prescription lenses in vintage sunglasses?

Most full-rim vintage frames can take prescription lenses.

Should I keep the original lenses?

Yes, especially branded glass lenses; they add value for collectors.
