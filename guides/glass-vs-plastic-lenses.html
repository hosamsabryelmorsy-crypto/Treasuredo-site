Glass or Plastic Lenses in Vintage Sunglasses: How to Tell and Which Is Better

Glass lenses are heavier, feel cool to the touch, resist scratches better and give very clear optics, but they can chip or break if dropped. Plastic lenses are lighter and more impact-resistant, but scratch more easily. Many vintage sunglasses up to the 1980s used glass lenses, including the well-known G-15 lenses of Ray-Ban; most modern sunglasses use plastic or polycarbonate.

How to tell glass from plastic

  1. Touch: glass feels cold against your cheek or lip; plastic warms quickly to room temperature.
  2. Weight: a pair with glass lenses feels noticeably heavier, especially with large lenses.
  3. Tap test: tap the lens gently with a fingernail; glass gives a sharper, higher click than plastic.
  4. Edges: glass edges are often slightly greenish or show small chips; plastic edges look smooth and can show tiny scratches.
  5. Markings: some vintage glass lenses carry a maker's logo etched near the edge.

Pros and cons

  • Glass: excellent clarity and scratch resistance; heavier; can chip at the edges or break on impact.
  • CR-39 plastic: the standard plastic lens since the mid-20th century; light, good optics, scratches more easily than glass.
  • Polycarbonate: very impact-resistant, the usual choice for sport; softer surface without a hard coating.

What to check on vintage lenses

  • Scratches: hold the lens against a light at an angle; scratches in the centre of vision matter more than at the edges.
  • Chips: on glass, look along the edge for small chips, especially near the rim screw.
  • Coatings: mirror and anti-reflective coatings can peel or craze; this cannot be repaired.
  • Tint match: both lenses should be the same colour and darkness; compare against white paper.
  • Distortion: look through each lens at a straight line, like a door frame, and move it slowly; the line should not wobble.

UV protection

Darkness does not equal UV protection. Many vintage glass sun lenses do block UV well, but the only way to be sure with an old pair is to have an optician check it with a UV meter, which takes a minute. If the lenses fail, new lenses with full UV protection can be cut to the original frame shape.

Should you keep original lenses?

For collectors, original lenses add value, especially branded glass ones. For daily wear, good replacement lenses can be lighter and safer. A sensible approach is to keep the original lenses stored safely and wear new ones.

G-15 and B-15: famous vintage glass lenses

The G-15 lens is a grey-green glass lens that lets through about 15% of visible light, giving natural colours with strong shade. The B-15 is its brown counterpart, with a little more contrast. Both were standard on many classic Ray-Ban models made by Bausch & Lomb, and finding them intact on a vintage pair is a plus.

Caring for each lens type

  • Glass: rinse grit off with water before wiping, because sand scratches even glass; store in a hard case to avoid chipping the edges.
  • Plastic: never wipe dry with a shirt or paper tissue; use a microfibre cloth and mild soapy water, and avoid alcohol or window cleaner, which can damage coatings.
  • Both: do not leave sunglasses in a hot car; heat damages coatings and can loosen lenses in acetate frames.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if my sunglasses lenses are glass?

Glass feels cold, is heavier, and gives a sharper click when tapped with a fingernail.

Are glass lenses better than plastic?

Glass resists scratches and gives very clear optics, but is heavier and can break; plastic is lighter and more impact-resistant.

Do vintage sunglasses protect against UV?

Many do, but darkness is not proof; an optician can test the lenses with a UV meter.

Can I replace vintage glass lenses?

Yes, an optician can cut new glass or plastic lenses to the original frame shape.
