Vintage "Made in France" Sunglasses: French Frame Makers and How to Identify Them

Vintage French sunglasses were mostly made in the Jura mountains of eastern France, around the towns of Oyonnax and Morez, which have been centres of French eyewear for generations. You can usually identify them by a "Made in France", "France" or "Fait main" (handmade) marking on the temple, along with a maker's or brand name, a model number and a size. Names collectors search for include independent makers such as Jean Lempereur and Henry Jullien, and many smaller brands whose frames were made in the same workshops.

Markings to look for

  • Country: "Made in France", "France" or "Frame France" inside a temple.
  • Handmade: "Fait main" or "Handmade" indicates a frame cut and finished by hand from acetate sheet.
  • Maker or brand: the workshop name or the brand it was made for.
  • Model and size: a model code plus lens width and bridge in millimetres, sometimes the temple length.
  • Star or decorative rivets: some French frames have visible pins on the front at the hinges, a detail many buyers look for.

Oyonnax and Morez

Oyonnax became known for plastics, from early celluloid to later acetate, and many classic French acetate frames come from this area. Morez is historically associated with spectacle making and metal frames. A French frame does not always say which town it came from, but thick, hand-polished acetate with deep, even colour is typical of the Jura tradition.

How to recognise a quality French acetate frame

  • Thickness and polish: edges should be smooth and rounded with a deep shine, not sharp or dull.
  • Colour depth: layered acetate shows colour through the thickness of the frame, visible on the edges.
  • Core wire: hold a temple up to light; a metal wire inside the acetate is normal and helps adjustment.
  • Hinges: riveted hinges, often with pins visible on the front, are stronger than glued-in ones.

Brands you will see on French-made frames

Besides independent makers, many fashion houses had their sunglasses made in France, including Christian Dior, Yves Saint Laurent, Nina Ricci, Rochas and Pierre Cardin. Smaller names such as Essel, Adensco, Jean de Paris, Diane de Carlo and Charles Jourdan also appear on French vintage frames. Unbranded frames marked only "France" can be just as well made, and often cost less.

Condition and age signs

Old celluloid frames, mostly from before the 1960s, can shrink or develop white crazing; acetate ages better but can still crack at the hinges. Check the frame front for warping by looking down at it from above: both lenses should sit on the same line. Slight shrinkage on very old frames can make lenses loose, which an optician can often fix with new lenses cut to fit.

General checks before you buy

  1. Hinges: open and close each temple; it should move smoothly with no wobble. Loose screws can be tightened, cracked hinge areas cannot.
  2. Frame material: look for hairline cracks, white haze or shrinkage, especially at the bridge and near the hinges.
  3. Lenses: check for scratches, peeling coatings or delamination at the edges. Sun lenses can be replaced, but original lenses add value.
  4. Size: compare lens width, bridge and temple length with a pair you already wear; a difference of 2-3 mm in lens width is noticeable.
  5. Straightness: place the frame upside down on a table; both temples should touch the surface evenly.

Frequently asked questions

Where are French sunglasses made?

Mostly in the Jura region of eastern France, around Oyonnax and Morez.

What does "Fait main" mean on sunglasses?

It is French for "handmade", meaning the frame was cut and finished by hand.

Are unbranded "Made in France" sunglasses good?

Many are made in the same workshops as branded frames; judge the acetate, hinges and lenses.

How can I tell celluloid from acetate?

Celluloid is mostly found on older frames and can show white crazing or shrinkage; acetate usually ages more evenly.
