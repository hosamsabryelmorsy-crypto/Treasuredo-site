Vintage Omega Constellation: Pie-Pan Dials, the Observatory Case Back and What to Check

The vintage Omega Constellation is Omega's chronometer line, launched in 1952. Collectors look for the faceted "pie-pan" dial, the observatory medallion on the case back and a chronometer-grade automatic calibre. A good one has an original dial, a medallion that is still sharp, and a movement that matches the dial (date or no date).

What makes it a Constellation

  • Chronometer movements: most vintage Constellations carry "Automatic Chronometer Officially Certified" on the dial.
  • Observatory medallion: the case back shows an observatory under stars. It is a key originality point.
  • Star on the dial: a small applied or printed star usually sits under the logo or near 6 o'clock.
  • Pie-pan dial: the outer ring of the dial slopes down in facets like the edge of a pie dish, mainly on 1950s-60s models.

Calibres by era

  • Early 1950s models use bumper automatics (the rotor swings and bumps rather than rotating fully).
  • Late 1950s models use the 500 series full-rotor automatics.
  • 1960s models use the 551 (no date) and 561 (date), both well regarded and serviceable.
  • Later models use 700 series and other calibres, and from the 1970s also quartz and Megaquartz.

If the dial has a date window, the calibre must be a date calibre. A non-date calibre in a date case means parts have been mixed.

Case shapes

1950s-60s Constellations often have "dog-leg" lugs, which step down in a curve. From the mid 1960s the C-shaped case, with a bold curved profile, became common. Polishing softens both shapes, so look for crisp edges in side-view photos.

How to avoid a redial

  1. Pie-pan dials are often repainted because originals are valuable. Check that the facets are even and the printing follows the slopes without smudging.
  2. "Chronometer" text should be sharp, evenly spaced and centred.
  3. Look for patina consistent with the rest of the watch; a flawless dial on a worn case is suspicious.
  4. Onyx or applied indices should all match in colour and height.

Case back medallion

The medallion is gold on many steel and gold-capped models. Polishing and wear can erase the stars and the observatory dome. A clear medallion adds value; a smooth one tells you the watch has been worn hard or polished. Inside the case back you should find Omega signing and a reference number.

Buying tips

  • Ask for a movement photo: the calibre number and Omega signing should be visible.
  • Check that the crown is signed with the Omega logo.
  • Expect to service an unserviced chronometer; it will only keep chronometer accuracy after a proper overhaul.

Steel, gold-capped or solid gold?

Vintage Constellations were made in stainless steel, gold-capped (a thick gold layer over a base metal) and solid gold. The case back markings tell you which one you are looking at. Gold-capped cases wear better than thin plating but can still show base metal at sharp edges after decades. Price comparisons only make sense between watches with the same case material, the same calibre and similar dial condition.

Frequently asked questions

When was the Omega Constellation introduced?

In 1952, as Omega's line of chronometer-certified automatic watches.

What is a pie-pan dial?

A dial whose outer edge slopes down in facets, like the rim of a pie dish. It is a hallmark of 1950s-60s Constellations.

What is the difference between Omega calibre 551 and 561?

Both are 1960s chronometer automatics; the 551 has no date and the 561 has a date.

What is on the back of a vintage Constellation?

An observatory medallion under stars. A sharp medallion suggests the case has not been heavily polished.
