How to Buy a Vintage Omega Seamaster (Calibres, Dials and Red Flags)

To buy a vintage Omega Seamaster safely, check four things: the dial is original (not repainted), the movement is the right Omega calibre for the model and era, the case back shows the seahorse emblem and the inside reference number, and the case has not been polished flat. Most Seamasters sold online are dress automatics from the 1950s-70s, not dive watches, and they are among the best-value vintage Omegas.

What "Seamaster" means on a vintage watch

  • Omega introduced the Seamaster in 1948, the company's 100th anniversary year.
  • Early Seamasters were water-resistant everyday watches with dress styling, not divers.
  • The Seamaster 300 (1957) started the professional diving line.
  • Sub-lines include the Seamaster Cosmic and the Seamaster De Ville, which became its own De Ville collection in 1967.

Common calibres and what they tell you

  • Manual wind 600 series (for example cal. 601, 611, 613): simple, robust, common in 1960s Seamasters without "Automatic" on the dial.
  • Automatic 550 series (cal. 552, 562, 565): well regarded 1960s automatics; 562 and 565 have date.
  • Automatic 1000 series (cal. 1010, 1012, 1020, 1022): 1970s, with quickset date; 1020/1022 add the day.

The calibre number is stamped on the movement. If the dial says "Automatic Date" but the movement photo shows a manual-wind calibre, something is wrong.

Case back and reference number

Most vintage Seamasters have the seahorse (hippocampus) emblem on the case back. A seahorse that is worn flat shows heavy polishing or wear. Inside the case back you should find Omega signing and a reference number; the movement also carries a serial number, which helps place the watch in time. Ask for these photos if the listing does not show them.

How to spot a redial

  1. The Omega logo should be crisp, evenly printed and centred.
  2. "Swiss Made" at 6 o'clock should be small and neat, not oversized or crooked.
  3. Applied indices and logo should sit flat with no glue marks around them.
  4. Very clean dials paired with worn cases and movements deserve suspicion.

Franken watches

A "franken" mixes parts from different watches: a Seamaster dial in a case from another model, or a movement swapped in from something else. Compare the case shape, hands and crown with known examples of the same reference. Generic crowns and hands are the most common replacements after service.

Condition points that change the price

  • Sharp lug edges and visible case bevels add value; rounded lugs mean polishing.
  • Gold-capped or plated cases should not show brass at edges.
  • An acrylic crystal can be polished or replaced cheaply; dial damage cannot be fixed without lowering value.
  • A recent service by a watchmaker is worth more than a perfect bracelet.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • When was it last serviced, and was the movement fully overhauled or only cleaned?
  • Can you show the inside of the case back and the movement with the calibre number?
  • Are the crown, hands and crystal original, or replaced during service?
  • Does it keep time in different positions (face up, crown down)? A healthy 1960s Omega automatic should run within a minute or so per day after service.

A seller who answers these clearly, with photos, is usually selling an honest watch.

Frequently asked questions

When did Omega launch the Seamaster?

In 1948, Omega's 100th anniversary year. Early models were water-resistant dress watches rather than dive watches.

Which vintage Seamaster movement is best?

The 1960s 550-series automatics (such as 552 and 562) and the 600-series manual calibres are reliable and widely serviceable.

How can I tell if a Seamaster dial is original?

Check that the printing is sharp and evenly spaced, the logo is centred and the indices sit cleanly with no glue marks.

Is a vintage Seamaster a dive watch?

Usually not. Most vintage Seamasters are dress automatics; only lines like the Seamaster 300 were built for diving.
