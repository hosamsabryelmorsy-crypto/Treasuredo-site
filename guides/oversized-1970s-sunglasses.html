Oversized 1970s Sunglasses: How to Identify a Real Vintage Pair

Genuine oversized 1970s sunglasses usually have lenses of 56-64 mm, gradient tints, and often temples attached low on the frame front, with makers' or fashion-house markings and a country of origin inside the temple. The look came from fashion houses and European frame makers, so the markings, materials and construction are what separate a real 1970s pair from a modern copy.

Design details typical of the 1970s

  • Large lenses: 56 mm and up, sitting high on the face, often reaching the cheekbones.
  • Low-set temples: on many frames the temples join the front near the middle or lower edge rather than the top corner.
  • Gradient lenses: dark at the top fading to light, in brown, grey, pink or green.
  • Shapes: big squares, octagons, butterflies and soft aviators.
  • Materials: thick acetate in tortoise, honey, burgundy and translucent colours, or thin gold-tone metal.

Markings to look for

  • Brand or maker: fashion houses such as Christian Dior, Yves Saint Laurent, Nina Ricci, Rochas and Pierre Cardin licensed oversized designs; makers such as Safilo and German firms produced them too.
  • Country: "Made in France", "Italy", "Germany" or "Austria" are common on 1970s frames.
  • Model, colour and size inside the temples, often worn from use.

Vintage or modern copy?

  1. Hinges: vintage acetate frames usually have riveted hinges, with pins visible on the front; cheap modern copies often have hinges pressed into thin plastic.
  2. Weight and thickness: genuine acetate feels dense and has polished, rounded edges.
  3. Lenses: many 1970s pairs have glass lenses; modern copies almost always use thin plastic.
  4. Wear: even plating or polish wear in contact areas is consistent with age; perfect markings on a worn frame are worth questioning.
  5. Proportions: modern "70s style" frames often have wider bridges and higher temples than originals.

Sizing oversized frames

Check lens width and total front width. A 58-60 mm lens with a narrow 14-16 mm bridge suits a narrow-bridged nose; a wider bridge sits lower. If you can, measure a pair you already wear from hinge to hinge across the front and compare. Low-set temples change how the frame sits; they let the lenses ride higher on the face, which is part of the 1970s look.

Condition checks for large lenses

Large gradient lenses show uneven fading and coating wear easily; compare the two lenses against white paper. Big acetate fronts can warp over time; place the frame face-down on a table to see whether it rocks. Check the bridge, the area under the most stress on heavy frames, for hairline cracks.

General checks before you buy

  1. Hinges: open and close each temple; it should move smoothly with no wobble. Loose screws can be tightened, cracked hinge areas cannot.
  2. Frame material: look for hairline cracks, white haze or shrinkage, especially at the bridge and near the hinges.
  3. Lenses: check for scratches, peeling coatings or delamination at the edges. Sun lenses can be replaced, but original lenses add value.
  4. Size: compare lens width, bridge and temple length with a pair you already wear; a difference of 2-3 mm in lens width is noticeable.
  5. Straightness: place the frame upside down on a table; both temples should touch the surface evenly.

Frequently asked questions

What lens size counts as oversized?

On vintage sunglasses, lens widths of about 56 mm and up are usually described as oversized.

Why are temples low on 1970s sunglasses?

Low-set temples were a design feature that lets large lenses sit higher on the face.

How can I tell real 1970s sunglasses from a copy?

Look at riveted hinges, dense polished acetate, glass lenses and genuine maker markings.

Can old gradient lenses be replaced?

Yes, an optician can cut new gradient sun lenses to the original shape.
