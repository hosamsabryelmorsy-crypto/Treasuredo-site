Vintage Zodiac Watches: Sea Wolf, Olympos, Astrographic and How to Buy One

The vintage Zodiac models collectors search for most are the Sea Wolf dive watch, the faceted Olympos dress watch and the Astrographic with its "floating" hands. A good example has an original dial, an unpolished case with crisp edges, the correct signed crown and a movement that runs well. Those four checks matter more than the model name when you judge value.

A short history that helps you date a Zodiac

  • Zodiac is a Swiss brand from Le Locle, founded in 1882 by Ariste Calame.
  • The Sea Wolf arrived in 1953, making it one of the earliest purpose-built dive watches sold to the public.
  • In the 1960s Zodiac made its own automatic movements, which is why many vintage pieces say "Zodiac" on the rotor and bridges rather than a supplier's name.
  • The Astrographic (late 1960s) shows the time with hands printed on clear discs, so they appear to float over the dial.
  • The brand was later bought by Fossil, and modern Sea Wolf reissues exist, so check you are looking at a vintage piece and not a recent one.

Sea Wolf: what to check

  • Bezel insert: original inserts fade and scratch; a bright, perfect insert on a worn case is usually a replacement. Replacement is not a deal-breaker but should lower the price.
  • Lume: 1950s-60s dials used radium or tritium that ages to cream or brown. Fresh white lume on hands or dial means relumed parts.
  • Case back and crown: look for Zodiac signing on both; generic crowns are common after service.
  • Water resistance: treat any vintage diver as not waterproof until a watchmaker has pressure-tested it with new gaskets.

Olympos: what to check

The Olympos is recognised by its angular, faceted case and dial treatment. The facets are what make it, so polishing that rounds them off hurts value badly. Check the crystal: the shaped crystals are harder to replace than a plain dome, so chips and deep cracks matter. Gold-plated cases should be checked at lug tips and case edges for brass showing through.

Astrographic: what to check

The floating hands sit on transparent discs. Look for scratches, yellowing or misalignment of the discs, and make sure the minute and seconds markers point exactly at the indices. These parts are specific to the model, so damaged discs are costly to put right.

Spotting a redial or "franken" Zodiac

  1. Zoom in on the dial printing: blurred edges, uneven spacing or thick fonts suggest a repaint.
  2. Check the movement photo: an unsigned or mismatched movement inside a Zodiac case is a warning sign.
  3. Compare the hands with other examples of the same model; replacement hands are common.
  4. Ask for the case back inside photo if it is not in the listing.

Which Zodiac to start with

Dress automatics with plain dials are the easiest entry and the cheapest to keep running. Sea Wolf and Astrographic models carry a premium, so pay that only for original dials and bezels. Whatever the model, a recent service matters more than a few cosmetic marks.

Frequently asked questions

When was the Zodiac Sea Wolf introduced?

The Sea Wolf was introduced in 1953 and is one of the earliest dive watches made for the general public.

Did Zodiac make its own movements?

Yes. In the 1960s Zodiac produced its own automatic calibres, and many vintage pieces carry Zodiac signing on the movement.

Is a vintage Zodiac Sea Wolf waterproof?

Not reliably. Any vintage diver needs new gaskets and a pressure test before it goes near water.

What lowers the value of a vintage Zodiac?

Repainted dials, replaced bezel inserts, heavily polished cases and non-original crowns or hands.
